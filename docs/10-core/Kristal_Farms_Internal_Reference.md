# Kristal Farms Internal Reference Document

## Overview and Purpose

Kristal Farms is a **cold-climate distributed compute infrastructure model** whose first detailed architecture is a hydro-powered, village-adjacent deployment. The core inversion is to **bring modular compute to renewable energy that is difficult or expensive to export, consume that energy locally, export digital value by fiber, and reuse server heat locally before rejecting it**. The first architecture places data containers near village heat users and port/fibre infrastructure, using short local electrical connections rather than making long-distance high-voltage transmission the default solution. Tenants lease “black-box” compute pads that are plug-ready with power, cooling, fiber and physical security while keeping control of their internal servers, software, data, models and logs. The sections below define the detailed community-integrated hydro architecture, governance, operations and phasing; the broader v3 platform vision also evaluates coastal/subsea fibre, secure brownfield/decommissioned-mine site variants, and a repeatable Québec–Labrador northern compute network. All such extensions remain subject to site-specific engineering, environmental, community, regulatory, security and economic validation.

### Strategic platform scope

The village-integrated hydro design in this document is the **baseline site archetype**, not the only possible future form of Kristal Farms. The platform strategy is to develop common technical and governance interfaces that can be reused across several site classes:

- **community-integrated nodes**, where heat recovery and local benefits are central;
- **coastal connectivity nodes**, where marine logistics and potential subsea-fibre access may add value;
- **secure brownfield or decommissioned-mine nodes**, investigated separately for workloads requiring stronger physical protection;
- **regional nodes**, considered only where energy, fibre, environmental conditions, community/rights processes and economics support larger scale.

The working hypothesis is that local energy consumption plus fibre export can, at some sites, avoid part of the cost and footprint associated with long electrical transmission corridors. This is a comparison to be proven, not a universal result. Likewise, direct seabed fibre access, mine-based security and future AI demand are strategic research tracks rather than assumed project conditions.

## Infrastructure Design and Architecture

### Energy Supply (Local Hydro Power Integration)

Kristal Farms taps into a **local hydroelectric source** to power the on-site compute pads. A **short medium-voltage (MV) feeder line** runs from the existing hydro plant to a new substation located in the village (preferably at the port or village edge). This avoids any long high-voltage transmission corridors or remote substations – **no new long HV lines** are needed, reducing losses and permitting requirements. From the village substation, **feeders distribute power** to each compute pad (container) and to the central heating equipment (pumps, heat exchanger station). Keeping the energy center in the village means the waste heat from servers is right where it’s needed – near radiators and the greenhouse, rather than at a remote dam.

Each pad’s electrical hookup includes a **metered power handoff** at the appropriate voltage level (MV or stepped-down LV). Power quality is monitored (voltage levels, harmonics, events) to protect sensitive IT equipment. The design calls for **coordinated start/stop sequencing** of pads to avoid large inrush currents or flicker on the local grid when servers power up or down. Likewise, each feeder has proper grounding and protective devices with selective coordination, ensuring that a fault in one pad will trip its feeder without taking down the whole site.

The **existing diesel generators** in the village are retained only as an emergency backup for critical loads and rare outage events. Under normal operation, the diesel is not used – the hydro plant and compute operations supply the village’s needs. If there were a major grid issue, critical infrastructure (perhaps the clinic or the NOC) could still be supported by diesel power, but the goal is to minimize diesel runtime. Similarly, for cooling continuity, **backup dry coolers** are installed (see below) so that if the primary heat-reuse cooling loop is unavailable, the IT load can be safely curtailed without overheating servers. Fiber connectivity also has path protection to keep data flowing (discussed in a later section).

**Load management** is implemented to align with the heat-first approach. The project will **stage the addition of compute pads in phases**, matching the IT load (kW) to confirmed heat sinks capacity in the community. In other words, new pads are powered on only when there are enough buildings or greenhouse demand to use their waste heat. Moreover, compute workloads can be **shaped to follow heat demand** – for example, running batch jobs harder during cold periods or when heat storage is low, and throttling back when the heat loop is saturated. The reuse→store→reject hierarchy is encoded in operating procedures (and in tenant contracts) so that lower-priority compute can be curtailed before any critical public heating is shorted. This ensures that **community heating needs always take priority** over maximizing compute output.

Electrical distribution losses are kept low by the short distances. The MV feeder from the dam to the village is much shorter than a typical transmission line, and there is **no need for new high-voltage step-ups or long-distance lines**. Every interface is metered – from the hydro plant output, into the substation, and out to each pad – to allow an auditable accounting of losses and usage across the system. For safety and compliance, the project enforces strict lockout/tagout procedures at the substation and pad connections, and conducts **routine tests before each seasonal ramp-up** (e.g. heading into winter, test the entire heating and electrical system under load).

### Cooling and Heat Recycling System

A central tenet of Kristal Farms is the **“heat-first” design**: **always reuse waste heat before considering rejection**. The cooling infrastructure is therefore built as a **heat recycling system** that captures server heat and delivers it for local use. Servers are not cooled by evaporative towers or chillers; instead, they use a **closed liquid loop** that runs through a **plate heat exchanger** to transfer heat into a district heating loop for the village. There are **two separate fluid circuits** – the IT cooling loop and the building heating loop – which are *thermally* connected but *hydraulically isolated* via heat exchangers (i.e. **non-contact heat exchange** with **no mixing** of fluids). This non-negotiable design feature prevents any contamination: the water that goes into homes or the environment never touches the water inside server racks.

**Cold source for cooling:** The primary cooling medium is the natural cold water available at the site. Typically, this is **seawater or bay water at the port**, which provides a large heat sink especially in winter months. Servers heat up the IT coolant to about 45–60 °C; this hot coolant goes through a plate heat exchanger where it gives off heat to the building loop, and then the now-cooled IT fluid (around 30–45 °C after exchanging heat) is sent to a second exchanger that dumps any excess heat into the bay water before returning to the servers. The bay water and IT water also do not mix – a **titanium plate HX** handles that exchange to avoid corrosion or biofouling issues. In summer, when building heat demand is low, more of the server heat will go out to the bay (still respecting temperature limits, as discussed below). **Backup dry coolers** are included as a secondary cold source for shoulder seasons or rare situations – these are radiator-like air-cooled units that can reject heat to ambient air if, for example, the bay intake is temporarily unavailable or the heat reuse capacity is saturated. The system is designed *not* to depend on small rivers or other fragile water sources as the main sink, to avoid ecological disturbance.

**Heat distribution (building loop):** On the other side of the IT heat exchanger is the **village heating loop**. This is a loop of water that circulates through the community to deliver heat to buildings and other uses. It uses **pre-insulated twin pipes** buried over short distances (since the village and pad yard are co-located). Each building that receives heat has a **substation** – essentially a heat interface unit with its own plate heat exchanger, control valves, and a heat meter. The building loop water transfers heat to the building’s own heating system through that exchanger, again keeping systems separate. The flow through each building substation is balanced by design (using differential pressure control valves, etc.) to ensure uniform heat distribution and avoid some buildings hogging flow. The **supply temperature** in the building loop is adjusted based on outside air temperature (an outdoor-reset control curve) to provide just enough heat to meet demand without overheating buildings.

The system can provide **low-temperature heat (45–60 °C)** directly from servers for buildings that can accept those temperatures (for example, buildings with oversized radiators, floor heating, or fan-coil units). In cases where **higher temperatures (65–75 °C)** are needed – such as buildings with legacy radiator systems sized for boiler water – the project includes a **central heat pump booster**. This booster will take the loop water and raise its temperature to the required level. It is sized for the peak winter heating load of the priority buildings (e.g., the school, clinic). Only when necessary will the booster run; otherwise, the system tries to operate in low-temperature mode for efficiency. Even domestic hot water (DHW) can be supplied by this system: the DHW in each building is preheated via the heat exchanger (substation), though final electric or gas top-up may be used to meet health code requirements (e.g., Legionella prevention by occasional high-temperature flushing).

**Seasonal heat use strategy:** In winter, virtually all server heat can be reused. The **priority sinks in winter are public buildings** – such as the clinic, school, and town hall – because they have steady heating needs. Once those are satisfied, any additional heat can go to nearby homes on the network. In the **shoulder seasons** (spring, autumn), heating demand in buildings drops, so the plan is to **mix building heating with greenhouse heating** and possibly charge thermal storage to buffer the daily fluctuations. In **summer**, the community’s building heating demand is minimal, so a **greenhouse** is used as the main heat sink to absorb the servers’ output. The greenhouse can use the heat for horticulture (extending the growing season or even year-round growth in a subarctic climate). Only if both the greenhouse **and** the thermal storage are unable to take more heat will the system resort to rejecting heat to the environment. This embodies the reuse → store → reject rule: every bit of heat is first tried to be put to productive use (heating buildings or greenhouse), then second priority is charging the **thermal storage tanks**, and only as a last resort is it dumped via the bay/dry coolers.

**Thermal storage:** To balance timing mismatches between when servers produce heat and when buildings need heat, the project uses **stratified thermal storage tanks**. In Phase 1, at least one tank is installed, sized for roughly \~6 hours of average heat load (this might be a tank that can hold on the order of tens of cubic meters of hot water). The stratification allows hotter water to stay at the top and cooler at the bottom, improving storage efficiency. These tanks are charged when heat production exceeds immediate demand (e.g., midday if all buildings are warm, or overnight if loads are low) and discharged during peak demand times (morning cold snaps, etc.). This **short-term storage smooths diurnal peaks** and gives the operators flexibility to **schedule compute tasks when heat is most needed** (for example, run more jobs in early morning to help warm buildings up). In future phases, if needed, the project could explore **seasonal storage** like borehole thermal energy storage (BTES) or pit thermal storage to carry excess summer heat into winter, though initially the plan is to focus on shorter-term storage.

**Controls and safeguards:** The entire heat recycling system is managed by smart controls. An **outdoor-reset control loop** adjusts the target supply temperature of the building heating loop based on outside weather, which is a typical energy-saving measure. There are also **ΔT limits set on any heat rejection**: the difference in temperature between intake and outflow for the bay water is kept below an agreed threshold to protect aquatic life. Sensors will trigger alarms if the discharge water approaches the ΔT limit or if any temperatures/pressures move out of range. In practice, this means if the servers are too hot and not enough heat is being taken by the community, the system will throttle the IT load or activate dry coolers to ensure the water returned to the bay is only a few degrees warmer than when it was taken in (as permitted by environmental regulators). The **Environment Committee** (discussed later) oversees compliance with these limits, and the system is designed to target **100% compliance** (no hours above the ΔT cap).

From an ecological perspective, the design aims to **minimize environmental impact**. By using an existing hydro site, no new flooding or dams are needed (the project doesn’t create any new reservoir, it just utilizes some capacity of an existing one). The siting of the pad yard on **low-value land** (e.g., near the port on previously disturbed or low ecological value ground) avoids disturbing pristine wilderness. There are **no evaporative cooling towers**, so **Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE) is near zero** – essentially no water is consumed for cooling aside from minor top-ups in closed loops. Reusing heat means fewer diesel generators running, which translates to less exhaust emissions (lower NOₓ and particulate pollution) and fewer fuel spills risk in the community. The reduction in diesel also directly reduces greenhouse gas emissions, a fact that will be tracked and reported (see Metrics section). Noise and light are kept to a minimum with a **compact site and directed lighting** at the port yard, mitigating nuisance to villagers and wildlife.

**Monitoring and acceptance:** The performance of the heat recycling system is closely watched through a handful of metrics. Chief among them is the **Heat Utilization Factor (HUF)** – the percentage of available server heat that is actually reused for useful purposes. The project sets a target floor for HUF (for example, a minimum seasonal average agreed with the community) and monitors it continuously. Another key metric is **ΔT compliance**, i.e. the percentage of time the cooling discharge stays within the environmental ΔT limit (target 100% of hours within limit). The **useful heat delivered** to the community (in MWh of thermal energy) is measured and broken down by sink (buildings, greenhouse, storage), to show how much heating value the project is providing. These figures are published on the dashboard (more in Metrics section) and used to verify that the **heat-first rule is being achieved in practice**.

Before the heat system goes fully live, a few **acceptance criteria** must be met. The non-contact cooling loop is pressure-tested and run to ensure absolutely **no cross-contamination** between the IT coolant and the community water. The **ΔT guard** (alarm and control system) is verified by simulating conditions to ensure it will catch any excessive temperature rise. **100% ΔT compliance** should be demonstrated in a trial run (e.g., during commissioning tests) before proceeding. The **HUF** should reach at least the minimum agreed value in test scenarios (for instance, if it’s summer, they ensure the greenhouse can take heat; if winter, that enough heat load exists to use the server output). The **heat pump booster** must be shown to deliver the required high temperature – for example, during a cold design-day test, the booster should enable legacy radiators to hit their setpoints. Only once these conditions are satisfied is the heat recycling system considered fully operational in service of the community.

### Compute Pads and Modular Design

The **compute pads** (data center modules) are deployed in a fenced yard at the port (or edge of the village), not up at the remote dam site – this ensures the heat they generate stays near the community where it can be used. Each pad is essentially a standard 40‑ft shipping container (or similar modular unit) that can be lifted directly from a ship at the quay and placed onto a prepared pad site. The pad yard is adjacent to the substation and the heat exchange facility, keeping the power lines and pipe runs short and efficient.

Each pad comes with **standard interfaces** provided by the host. There is a power handoff (medium-voltage to low-voltage conversion as needed, delivered through a pad-mounted PDU with a dedicated meter), a cooling interface (the pad’s internal liquid cooling loop connects via a **plate heat exchanger** to the communal building heat loop, without mixing fluids), and dual fiber uplink ports that tie into the NOC’s network switches. These plug-and-play interfaces mean any compliant data container can be quickly installed or removed. The tenant’s equipment inside the container is cooled by its own closed loop, which only exchanges heat through the plate exchanger – this non-contact design maintains the isolation between the IT equipment coolant and the village water loop.

In the **black-box tenancy** approach, the host’s responsibility stops at those physical interfaces. The tenant manages everything inside the container (servers, operating systems, data, etc.), and the host has no visibility beyond external metrics like power draw, coolant flow/ΔT, heat output, and network link status. This modular approach allows incremental scaling: Phase 1 might start with one or two pads, and as compute demand grows (and as more heat sinks become available to absorb the waste heat), additional pads can be added in Phase 2 and beyond. Because each pad is a self-contained module, there is also inherent **physical reversibility** – a pad can be removed when a lease ends or if the project winds down, and the site can be restored to its original state with minimal lasting impact.

The design includes provisions for **maintenance and continuity**. The host will schedule planned maintenance windows for power, cooling, or fiber systems that affect the pads, communicating these to tenants and ensuring they occur during low-impact periods. Pad start-up and shut-down sequences are coordinated to avoid electrical surges (e.g. staggering the energization to prevent inrush currents that could cause voltage flicker on the local grid). For unplanned issues, redundancies are in place: if the primary cold source (seawater loop) is unavailable or a pad’s cooling needs to be curtailed, **dry coolers** serve as a backup to dissipate heat and allow an orderly IT load reduction without overheating. Likewise, the existing diesel generator remains as an emergency power backup for critical loads in the village, meaning essential services and potentially some baseline pad functions can continue during rare outages. The operations team tracks pad utilization and health (e.g. percentage of IT capacity used and pad uptime/downtime) as part of the overall metrics, to ensure each module is performing reliably.

### Fiber Connectivity and Network Operations

**Fiber connectivity** is critical to Kristal Farms, because instead of exporting electricity, the site exports data from computation. The project establishes a small **Network Operations Center (NOC)** at the port site to house networking equipment. From the NOC, a main fiber **trunk line** runs southward to the regional hub (e.g., Goose Bay), leveraging Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing (DWDM) technology to carry many high-capacity channels on a single fiber pair. Within the village, short fiber **feeder lines** run from the NOC to each server pad and to local facilities like the clinic or school. Each pad is provided with dual independent fiber uplinks (A/B) connecting to separate switches or routes, so that if one link fails the other can maintain connectivity. Where feasible, the trunk itself is laid out with physically diverse paths or rings to ensure path protection – meaning a fiber cut on one route won’t isolate the site because traffic can switch to the alternate path. All fiber cables, panels, and ports are systematically labeled for clear management, and an Optical Distribution Frame (ODF) in the NOC provides a structured way to patch and test connections.

**Network Operations:** The network is monitored and managed to provide high reliability. The NOC has redundant power supplies (A/B feeds) for its critical equipment. The system continuously tracks metrics such as link availability (uptime percentage), latency (especially the 95th percentile latency to the hub), jitter (delay variation), and error rates. Automated failover is configured both at the pad level and on the backbone: if a pad’s primary link goes down, traffic will automatically switch to the secondary link within moments. If a major trunk failure occurs, the path-protected architecture will likewise reroute the traffic. Quality of Service (QoS) rules are in place to differentiate traffic types: batch AI training jobs use a default class, whereas interactive services (e.g., any real-time applications) go into dedicated low-latency queues, and a portion of bandwidth is reserved for community services like the clinic and school. The host strictly upholds the black-box principle in networking as well: there is no Deep Packet Inspection of tenant data, and the host can only see metadata (such as whether a link is up, or general bandwidth usage) but not the content of any packets. All NOC access is controlled and logged, and any network changes (new splices, patching, configuration changes) go through a change control procedure to avoid accidental outages. The public dashboard for Kristal Farms includes network performance indicators – for example, aggregate pad link uptime, the trunk’s availability percentage, and the current latency – as well as counts of how many local sites are connected via the network. Acceptance tests for the connectivity system require that the trunk link meets its SLA (e.g., proved high uptime and low latency in a test period), that each pad demonstrated seamless dual-link failover, that tenant network traffic is kept completely separate from local community traffic, and that the network monitoring dashboard is live before moving to full operation.

## Tenancy and Governance

### Black-Box Tenancy Model

Kristal Farms uses a **“black-box” tenancy model** for the compute pads. This means the **host provides the utilities (power, cooling, heat export, fiber connectivity, and physical security) up to the pad, but has no access to the tenant’s inside operations or data**. The pad and its servers are essentially an opaque box from the host’s perspective. The tenant installs and controls their own hardware, operating system, software, models, and data within that container. The host **only** monitors *physical infrastructure metrics* – for example, the energy the pad consumes (kWh), the cooling loop flow rate and temperature difference (ΔT) across the heat exchanger, whether the pad is online (heartbeat pings or power draw), network link status, and basic alarms (like if temperature in the pad goes too high or if a fiber link drops). **No tenant logs, application data, or content are accessible to the host** – there is no inspection of network payloads (no packet sniffing beyond aggregate bandwidth usage), no reading of tenant drive data, and no intrusion into the container’s internal telemetry. All communications can be encrypted end-to-end by the tenant, and the host’s networking equipment does not perform Deep Packet Inspection, aligning with the black-box rule.

At the time a pad is turned up for a new tenant, there is an option to perform a **hardware attestation** (using Trusted Execution Environment enclaves or similar technology) to prove that the pad’s servers are in a known secure state. This is **offered but not required by default** – if a tenant’s workloads are extremely sensitive, they can request an attested environment in their lease for additional security assurances, but otherwise the standard black-box (with encryption and isolation but without a formal TEE attestation) is assumed. In all cases, tenant data confidentiality is paramount: **the host cannot inspect tenant data, models, code, or logs**, and **any data that is reported publicly is aggregated and anonymized** (no personal or tenant-specific info).

The host also **cannot unilaterally interfere with the tenant’s workloads** beyond the infrastructure level. The only exceptions are for **safety or agreed-upon “demand shaping”** related to the heat-first policy. For instance, the host may curtail or throttle some non-critical compute tasks if there is an urgent need to reduce heat output (to stay within environmental limits), but such conditions are predefined in contracts and would never involve accessing the data itself – just sending a signal to reduce power consumption. Tenants otherwise have full autonomy over how they use their allocated IT capacity.

### Lease Agreements and Service Terms

Each compute pad is leased under a straightforward contract that specifies the capacity and interfaces provided. For example, the lease defines the **IT power capacity** in kW (and power quality standards), the **cooling interface** parameters (e.g. supply/return temperature and flow rates on the pad’s side of the heat exchanger), the number of **fiber ports** provided, and the on-site **yard rules** for the pad (physical access, security, etc.). The lease includes a **Service Level Agreement (SLA)** committing to infrastructure availability and support: the host guarantees a certain uptime percentage and maximum response time for outages, and maintenance windows are specified to coordinate any planned downtime. Crucially, a **heat-first clause** is built in, requiring the tenant to cooperate with heat reuse and curtailment priorities (i.e. they agree that the host can direct waste heat to local uses, and if needed, request non-essential workloads to pause to ensure heat can be used or stored). If the host fails to meet the SLA targets (for power, cooling, or network availability), the contract provides for **penalties or credits** to compensate the tenant. Conversely, if a tenant’s operation endangers the system (for example, if they exceed their power draw cap or refuse to connect to the heat loop), the host has defined **step-in rights** to intervene for safety reasons. The lease also explicitly confirms the **reversibility** condition: at the end of the term, the infrastructure can be removed and the site returned to baseline, ensuring the tenant understands the pad is temporary on the land.

In addition to the pad lease itself, there are complementary agreements governing heat, network, and benefits:

* **Heat Supply Agreement:** Sets the temperature bands for heat delivery (e.g., whether low-temperature or boosted high-temperature heat will be provided), how heat output is metered, the curtailment order (i.e., which loads get priority if heat is limited), and a reserved quota of heat for social use (public buildings). It may also outline the basis for any heat tariffs and how disputes are handled.

* **Fiber Service Agreement:** Establishes the network service parameters – the target availability (uptime percentage) and acceptable latency (often specified as p95 latency), ensures there are protected fiber paths for redundancy, and requires that any maintenance affecting connectivity is communicated in advance. Essentially it formalizes the network SLA for connectivity to the pad.

* **Community Benefits Agreement (CBA/IBA):** Documents the commitments for local economic and social benefits: for instance, it might include targets or mechanisms for hiring local staff and contractors, providing training or internships, preferences for local procurement, the creation of a local community fund, and the schedule for reporting on these commitments. This agreement ensures that the village sees tangible benefits and has a say in how those are implemented.

### Community Engagement and FPIC

From the outset, Kristal Farms follows a **“community-first” engagement process**. It adheres to the principles of **Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC)** with the local Indigenous and non-Indigenous community. In practical terms, this means the project goes through stages of **informing** the community, **consulting** on plans and incorporating feedback, seeking formal **consent** before groundbreaking, then maintaining an ongoing dialogue as operations commence (monitoring and adjusting in response to community input), and conducting periodic **reviews** to renew or reassess consent. This FPIC process is not a one-time checkbox; it continues through the project’s life in the form of annual or biannual community review meetings, etc., to ensure the project retains its social license to operate.

Prior to initial power-on, **all necessary community agreements are put in place**. A condition for the project to proceed to operation is that **FPIC has been concluded positively and formalized**, and that a **Benefit Agreement (CBA/IBA)** with the community is signed. The Impact and Benefit Agreement typically covers things like local jobs, training, revenue sharing or community fund contributions, preferential contracts for local businesses, and guarantees of certain community heat allocations. These agreements are tailored to each site – rather than imposing a one-size-fits-all number of jobs or dollars, Kristal Farms works with each community to set realistic and mutually agreeable benefit targets. For example, instead of a fixed quota of local hires across all sites, the agreement might state an intention (and provide resources) to hire and train as many local residents as possible, with actual numbers and timelines to be adjusted per the community’s population and skills. This flexibility ensures commitments are meaningful and achievable in the local context, while still holding the project accountable to delivering benefits.

A portion of the benefits may be funneled into a **community development fund**. The principle of such a fund is agreed upon (the project would contribute financially, and the community would co-manage and audit the fund usage), but the **details are handled in the Community Benefits agreement itself** rather than the lease contract. This keeps the community benefit mechanisms clearly separated and under community oversight. The fund could support local initiatives like education, infrastructure, or cultural programs, and it would typically be overseen by a committee with community representation, ensuring transparency in how funds are spent.

Throughout the engagement, communication is kept transparent. **Public meetings** are held at key milestones, and **minutes** from those meetings are made available. The community has a formal **grievance mechanism** – essentially a right to raise concerns and seek remedies. For instance, if any community member or leader feels the project is not living up to its commitments or is causing unforeseen issues, they can bring this to the Project Council or an independent mediator as defined in the agreements. Clear timelines for responses are set, so concerns don’t linger without resolution. This approach aligns with best practices for Indigenous partnerships and local acceptance: *transparency, inclusivity, and accountability*.

### Governance Structure and Committees

Kristal Farms incorporates a multi-tier governance structure to oversee different aspects of the project, ensuring that the host, tenants, and community share appropriate responsibilities. Four main bodies are established:

* **Project Council:** This is the high-level steering committee for the project. It includes representatives from the **community**, the **public owner** (government or utility, if any), the **site operator** (the host company running Kristal Farms), the **tenant(s)** (major lessees of the compute pads), and the **greenhouse operator**. The Project Council’s mandate covers overall strategy, approving budgets, ensuring compliance with agreements, and resolving any major conflicts or disputes. In essence, it’s the forum where all key stakeholders come together, giving the community a voice alongside corporate and government voices at the decision-making table.

* **Heat Committee:** This committee focuses on the **thermal operations** and heat usage. It is responsible for setting **seasonal heat priorities** (for example, deciding each season how to allocate heat if there’s not enough for all demands, such as prioritizing the clinic and school in winter) and approving the list of heat sinks (e.g., deciding when the greenhouse should open its heat usage window). The Heat Committee also reviews the logs related to heat use, such as the Heat Utilization Factor (HUF) reports and any curtailment events where available heat exceeded what was reused. This committee likely includes local representatives (for example, someone from the town government or a local energy manager) as well as technical staff from the host. They ensure the **heat-first rule** is actively managed and adapted to conditions (like a particularly cold winter or a mild one, adjustments in greenhouse operations, etc.).

* **Environment Committee:** This body monitors **environmental compliance and impact**. It keeps an eye on the **ΔT of cooling water discharge**, ensuring the project stays within the agreed environmental limits. It also monitors flows, water quality (to ensure the cooling system isn’t adversely affecting the bay or river), and records any environmental incidents (such as an unplanned heat rejection or a spill) and how they were mitigated. The Environment Committee may include local environmental officers, representatives from indigenous groups concerned with land/water, and project engineers. If, for instance, there’s a period of low water flow in the river/bay, this committee would be involved in decisions to reduce IT load or otherwise respond. They are essentially guardians of the ecological aspects of Kristal Farms.

* **Kristals Council:** This committee governs the **Knowledge Commons initiative (Kristals)** associated with Kristal Farms. It selects which **public-interest topics** will be researched by the AI (for example, local health information, climate adaptation strategies, agricultural best practices), validates the quality and appropriateness of the answers generated, and approves them for publication to the community. The Kristals Council also oversees metrics related to the knowledge base – such as how often people use the Kristals, and ensuring the information stays up-to-date. It is composed of a mix of community members, domain experts (e.g., a healthcare professional for health topics, an educator for school topics), possibly a tenant representative (if the tenants are involved in computing the Kristals), and host representatives. This council ensures that the **black-box boundary** is respected in the knowledge project (only public data and consented topics are used) and that the outputs truly benefit the community.

These bodies are designed to be inclusive and transparent. They operate under charters that delineate which decisions they can make versus what they advise on (for instance, the Project Council likely has decision authority on budgets, whereas the Heat Committee might be advisory with recommendations on heat allocation). If any **social friction or conflict** arises, the structure allows it to be addressed through these channels – for example, if community members raise a concern about noise or an unmet commitment, the Project Council would take it up and could mediate a solution. All committees share information through the **public dashboard and scorecard**, and meetings may have open sessions or published summaries so the community can stay informed of discussions. This governance framework effectively means the **community helps run the project**: the host runs the technical utilities, the tenants run their compute, but the community (through these committees) runs the governance and ensures local benefits are delivered.

### Knowledge Commons (“Kristals”)

In parallel with its physical infrastructure, Kristal Farms includes an innovative digital commons known as **Kristals**. The idea is to **“recycle intelligence the way we recycle heat.”** Instead of every user or organization repeatedly querying large AI models (which consumes compute power each time), Kristal Farms will **save and share high-value AI-generated answers** on topics of public interest. These topics might include **health**, **ecology**, **local prosperity (economic development)**, **education**, and other issues important to the community. By curating these answers, Kristal Farms creates a durable knowledge asset for the community and avoids wasting compute on questions that have already been answered well.

A **“Kristal”** is a knowledge capsule. Each Kristal contains a clear question and a concise answer, along with references (sources used), the date, and information about how it was generated (which AI model, what parameters or prompts were used, and who validated it). It also carries a status (proposed → validated → published, etc.) and a unique identifier or hash to ensure integrity. All Kristals are versioned, so if new information emerges or an update is needed, a new version of the capsule is created with a change log. **Only public domain or consented information is included** – no private tenant data or personal information ends up in Kristals. For example, a Kristal might be *“What are the most effective traditional remedies for common colds in region X?”* answered with sources from public health research and local traditional knowledge (if shared), validated by local health experts, and made available for anyone to read.

The **workflow** for creating Kristals involves multiple steps to ensure quality and privacy: (1) The **Kristals Council** curates and selects topics that are in the public interest. (2) The question is clearly drafted and scoped (making sure it’s answerable and not infringing on privacy). (3) Compute is run – either using spare cycles on the Kristal Farms pads or other agreed resources – to generate the answer, usually by querying AI models with the relevant data. (4) The draft answer goes through a **validation process**: at least two reviewers check the facts, ensure there’s no bias or inappropriate content, and verify that sources are properly cited. (5) Any sensitive details (even if inadvertently included) are **redacted or anonymized** – personal identifiable information (PII) is removed, and the answer is kept general and privacy-respecting. (6) The Kristal is then **published** to a portal, with all its metadata (question, answer, sources, creation date, etc.). Finally, (7) over time the Kristals Council monitors usage and feedback – if facts change or new data comes in, they update or retire capsules as needed.

Community members and anyone interested can access the Kristals through a **web portal** for human reading or via an **API** for applications to query. The content is open (or at least accessible to the community; licensing can be decided per capsule, possibly open-source style or community-restricted). One could imagine a local school integrating the Kristals API to help students research local environmental issues, for example. The goal is that when someone has a question that has already been answered and vetted, the **“hit-rate”** of Kristals will be high – meaning they find their answer immediately in the knowledge base, saving the cost of recomputing it. This **“compute avoided”** (measured in kWh of IT that didn’t have to be spent because the answer was reused) is tracked as a metric of success. The Kristals initiative thus turns some of the compute power toward a public good, creating a **shared knowledge commons** that grows over time and benefits all users (while still respecting the confidentiality of any private tenant workloads).

From a governance standpoint, the **Kristals Council** as mentioned brings a multi-party oversight to this process. It not only picks the topics but also ensures the answers are understandable and relevant to the community (e.g., providing plain-language summaries when needed). It also sets a **review cadence** – Kristals might be audited periodically to ensure quality (especially as they plan to replicate the concept to other sites, sharing capsules between villages). The council ensures that the **black-box rule** is upheld: for example, if a tenant is doing proprietary AI research, none of that leaks into Kristals without permission; Kristals only use **approved public data and models** or perhaps open-source models provided by the host. The **public metrics** associated with Kristals (like hit-rate, compute avoided, number of capsules published, revision rates) are reported as part of the project’s overall scorecard.

In summary, Kristals add an extra dimension to Kristal Farms: beyond just selling compute and heat, the project also **generates knowledge value**. This is done in a way that respects privacy and benefits the community, creating a virtuous cycle where AI answers get better and more accessible the more they are used, without constantly re-investing compute resources to regenerate the same answers.

## Operations, Metrics, and Monitoring

### Key Performance Metrics

To track the success and impact of Kristal Farms, a **small set of key metrics** is defined (kept “small and stable” for simplicity and transparency). These metrics are reported publicly via a dashboard and regular reports. The major metrics include:

* **Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE):** This is the ratio of total power consumed by the facility to the power used by the IT equipment alone. It is an industry standard measure of data center energy efficiency. Kristal Farms targets a very efficient operation (low PUE), especially since the cold climate and absence of large cooling chillers reduce overhead. PUE is tracked in winter (when it will be lowest) and on an annual average.

* **Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE):** Measures how much water is used for cooling per unit of IT energy (often expressed in liters per kWh). At Kristal Farms, **WUE ≈ 0 by design**, because the cooling is done via closed-loop water-to-water heat exchangers with no evaporative cooling towers. Essentially, aside from minor topping up of the loops, water consumption is negligible. Reporting WUE \~0 confirms the project isn’t consuming freshwater for cooling needs.

* **Useful Heat Delivered:** The total amount of heat (thermal energy in MWh\_th) delivered to local **heat sinks** – primarily buildings and the greenhouse. This is often broken down by sink category (e.g., X MWh to public buildings, Y MWh to greenhouse, Z MWh stored). It shows how much benefit the community is getting in terms of heating. Ideally, this number is a large fraction of the servers’ waste heat output.

* **Heat Utilization Factor (HUF):** The percentage of total available waste heat from the servers that is actually put to productive use (the ratio of useful heat reused to total heat generated). A HUF of 100% would mean all server heat is reused; a lower HUF means some heat had to be dumped. Kristal Farms aims for as high a HUF as possible (with a minimum target agreed seasonally, e.g., a floor in summer vs winter). This metric encapsulates the efficiency of the heat-first system.

* **Diesel Avoided:** The project tracks how much diesel fuel use has been offset both in electricity generation and in heating. This can be expressed in liters of diesel saved, or converted to MWh of energy and even into tonnes of CO₂ equivalent emissions avoided. It includes two aspects: (1) diesel not burned for electricity because local hydro and servers are meeting needs, and (2) diesel not burned for heating because server heat is replacing boilers. This metric directly ties to environmental and cost benefits for the community (less fuel delivered and burned).

* **Pad Occupancy and Uptime:** “Pad occupancy” refers to the fraction of the IT capacity (compute power) that is actually in use by tenants on average. Uptime refers to the percentage of time the pad (and its provided services) is operational. High occupancy indicates strong utilization (and thus economic viability and more heat generation), while high uptime indicates reliability. These are typically aggregated across all pads, but could also be reported per pad if needed. The dashboard might show, for example, that the site has 10 MW of IT deployed and 8 MW are active (80% occupancy), and that uptime in the last quarter was 99.9% on power/network availability, etc..

* **Network Performance (Availability and Latency):** This covers the **fiber connectivity reliability** – typically reported as the **percentage of time the network is up** (both the main trunk and pad links) and the **latency (p95)** to the internet hub. For example, fiber availability might be 99.9% and the one-way latency 5 ms at 95th percentile. These ensure that compute jobs can be shipped in/out effectively. If there are any significant outages or high latency periods, those would show up in these metrics.

* **Community Benefit Metrics:** In line with the community-first approach, a few socio-economic indicators are tracked. These include **jobs and training opportunities** created for local people (e.g., number of local jobs filled, hours of training provided). The Kristals program provides metrics like **Kristals published** (the number of knowledge capsules available) and **Kristals hit-rate** (the percentage of knowledge queries that were answered by existing Kristals vs requiring new compute). Additionally, the project might track things like how much local produce the greenhouse yields or other community-specific outcomes, but the core ones enumerated focus on energy, environment, and digital knowledge benefits. All **public-interest publications or reuse of Kristals** are noted as well, showing the growth of the knowledge commons.

These metrics together provide a well-rounded view of performance: **energy efficiency** (PUE, WUE), **environmental impact** (HUF, heat delivered, diesel avoided), **reliability** (uptime, network stats), and **community value** (jobs, training, knowledge shared). By keeping the list limited and concrete, the project avoids information overload and focuses on outcomes that everyone can understand and care about. As noted in planning documents, *“These six numbers tell the entire economic story without drowning the reader in engineering.”* (In practice, the final dashboard might have a few more than six metrics once community and knowledge metrics are included, but the philosophy is to remain concise).

### Monitoring and Reporting

Kristal Farms employs continuous monitoring systems to collect data for the above metrics and to ensure smooth operations. All critical systems are instrumented: power meters at every handoff, temperature and flow sensors on loops, fiber link status monitors, etc. The **host’s operations center receives alerts** if something strays outside normal bounds – for instance, if the coolant ΔT is approaching the maximum allowed, or if a pad isn’t drawing power when it’s expected to (which could indicate an outage inside the pad). This allows proactive responses. The network is similarly monitored for packet loss or jitter, with alarms set on thresholds.

Importantly, **only non-intrusive data is monitored** – as described under black-box tenancy, it’s all about physical metrics and not tenant content. The monitoring data is logged in an immutable way (tamper-evident logs) to ensure trust. **Retention of logs**: The plan is to keep these physical metrics logs indefinitely for historical analysis, rather than purging them, since they do not contain sensitive personal data.

For transparency with the community and stakeholders, Kristal Farms maintains a **public-facing dashboard/scorecard**. This dashboard is updated in near real-time or at least on a **monthly** basis with the key metrics. For instance, each month it may publish the cumulative heat delivered, current HUF, current PUE, diesel savings, etc., perhaps via a website accessible to all. Additionally, a **summary display** could be placed in a local public place (like a monitor at the town hall) to show villagers the status of their “data farm” project. This proactive transparency helps build trust and keeps everyone informed of progress and any issues.

Beyond the dashboard, **formal reports** are generated. A **quarterly report** is prepared that dives a bit deeper into the data and is reviewed jointly with the governance committees (Heat, Environment, Project Council, etc.). In these quarterly reviews, the committees discuss performance, any incidents, and whether any changes in operation or policy are needed. Then, an **annual report** is published and presented at a public meeting, summarizing the year’s performance including not just technical metrics but also socio-economic indicators (jobs, local purchasing, etc.). This annual report provides a big-picture evaluation and is a tool for accountability, ensuring the project delivers on its promises over the long term.

All reports and dashboards maintain the rule of **no personal or sensitive data**. For example, if job numbers are reported, they might say “X local jobs” but not name individuals or share private info. If network uptime is reported, it doesn’t include anything about what data was transmitted. This way, **transparency is achieved without compromising privacy or security**.

In summary, the monitoring and reporting regimen of Kristal Farms ensures **operational excellence and community oversight**. Issues are caught early by sensors and alarms; the community sees the performance metrics and benefits through an accessible dashboard; and regular reports close the feedback loop by evaluating the project against its objectives and obligations.

### Risk Management and Incident Response

A robust risk management plan is in place, mapping out potential issues and corresponding remedies. Some key risk scenarios and responses include:

* **Heat Utilization Risk (Summer surplus):** During warm months, if **heat production exceeds what the community and greenhouse can absorb**, the system first makes full use of the greenhouse (which can dump heat via plant transpiration and ventilation) and charges any available thermal storage. Only if those are fully utilized does it open the **reject loop** to the bay, and even then, rejection is throttled to maintain ΔT compliance. This risk is mitigated by designing a sufficiently large greenhouse capacity for summer and possibly accepting a minor compute curtailment on extremely hot days. The Heat Committee monitors such situations, and logs any heat rejection events. In practice, this hierarchy ensures that **reuse is always maximized before any heat is wasted**.

* **Heating Demand Mismatch (Legacy Radiators in Winter):** In very cold conditions, or in buildings with old heating systems, there might be a need for hotter water than the servers alone provide. The mitigation is the **central heat pump booster** which can raise temperatures to 65–75 °С as needed. The booster is used primarily for the highest-priority buildings (e.g., a health center that absolutely must stay warm). To manage this risk, the booster is sized for the worst-case cold snap and its performance (COP – Coefficient of Performance) is tracked. The booster is considered to have passed acceptance when it can keep those buildings at setpoint on a design-cold day. Using the booster does impose a slight efficiency penalty (since it consumes power), so the Heat Committee may opt to reduce IT loads if heat is short, rather than run the booster at max for lower-priority heat loads. In short, **enable the booster and prioritize public buildings** in such cases.

* **Cooling Water or Environmental Incident:** If the **water supply for cooling is limited** (say a period of very low water levels or an environmental regulator imposes a restriction) or if the **ΔT limit is in danger of exceedance**, the plan includes several steps: **schedule maintenance** during these periods (to reduce heat output), **throttle the IT workload** (particularly any non-critical or batch jobs) to lower heat generation, or switch to **backup dry coolers** to dissipate heat into air instead of water. By reducing server activity, the heat output drops, protecting the environment. The Environment Committee would be involved in any such event, verifying that the responses (like throttling compute) were adequate to prevent ecological harm.

* **Fiber Cut / Network Outage:** Despite having redundant fiber paths, a major incident (for example, a backhoe inadvertently cutting a cable in the single common segment) could disrupt connectivity. In this case, the system’s design with automatic **failover** will immediately attempt to reroute traffic if any alternate path is available. The **NOC operators follow a runbook** to handle network incidents: they will quickly detect the fault (via link loss alarms and OTDR tests), **isolate the fault**, and notify tenants about the issue and expected time to restore. Spare fiber and splicing kits are kept on hand to expedite repairs. If the trunk is down, the site can continue to run batch jobs and buffer results, and critical interactive services might fail over to a backup satellite link if available (not mentioned explicitly in docs, but an example of strategy). The risk to mitigate here is loss of data export – the redundancy and quick response aim to keep any downtime minimal (tracked by MTTR metric). **Path protection** (diverse routing) is established wherever feasible to avoid single points of failure on the network.

* **Social or Community Conflict:** It’s recognized that any project can face **social risks**, such as community dissatisfaction or misunderstandings. Kristal Farms addresses this through its governance structure: if an issue arises (e.g., noise complaints, or concerns that promises aren’t being kept), the matter can be taken to the **Project Council** which includes community representatives, or even external mediation if needed. The presence of formal agreements (FPIC, CBA) and committees provides a clear escalation path. For instance, if the community feels the distribution of heat isn’t fair, the Heat Committee can discuss and adjust the priorities openly. If there is broader friction (say, the community wants more seats on the Project Council, or a dispute between a tenant and local business arises), the structure allows for **mediation and published actions** to resolve it. The project also tracks social indicators (like jobs delivered) and publicly reports them, so it’s less likely for grievances to fester unseen. In essence, **transparency and shared governance mitigate social risks**.

* **Tenant Non-Compliance or Safety Event:** Risks on the tenant side include a tenant attempting to use the infrastructure in a way that violates agreements – for example, refusing to curtail any load even when emergency heat reduction is needed, or running prohibited workloads. In such cases, the lease terms and governance provide remedies. Financial **penalties or service credits** can be applied if SLA or contractual obligations are not met (for instance, if a tenant doesn’t connect their cooling properly and rejects heat when they shouldn’t, they might incur a penalty). For severe violations or unsafe conditions, the host has the right to **step in and shut down a pad for safety reasons** – effectively cutting power or network until an issue is resolved, though this is a last resort measure. Additionally, tenants are likely subject to standard confidential computing audits at onboarding (attestation as mentioned) to ensure no malicious activity that could threaten others. The **“step-in for safety”** clause is crucial – it assures the community and regulators that the host can act decisively to prevent harm, even though tenants normally have free rein.

For each of these scenarios, **standard operating procedures (SOPs) or runbooks are in place**. The staff are trained with drills – for example, performing a simulated fiber cut to practice the response, or a controlled test of shutting down a pad if a heat alarm triggers, to see how quickly heat output drops. The project documentation outlines a “risk → remedy map” explicitly linking each risk to a mitigation strategy. Moreover, **independent audits** may be conducted annually to verify that the black-box data separation is intact and that the heat-first rule is being followed in operations (including ΔT compliance).

When incidents do occur, the response protocol emphasizes transparency and improvement. For any **major incident** – say a privacy breach (though that would more likely be on the tenant’s side) or an environmental limit exceedance – the host will issue an **immediate notification** to key parties (affected tenants, community reps, relevant authorities) within 24 hours. They then conduct a **Root Cause Analysis (RCA)**, usually concluding within \~48 hours, to understand exactly what happened. Once the issue is understood and contained, a **public incident report** is produced that explains the event and how it was resolved (without exposing any sensitive info). The incident and its resolution steps would be noted on the public dashboard (for instance, an annotation that on a given date a ΔT excursion occurred and was corrected). The governance committees, especially Environment or Project Council depending on the incident, would review the incident and make recommendations to prevent recurrence. This way, **lessons learned become part of the project’s knowledge base**, and stakeholders can see that issues are handled properly. The existence of a **“single window” grievance and incident register** ensures there’s traceability for any problem and its fix, reinforcing trust and accountability.

## Phasing and Implementation Roadmap

Kristal Farms is implemented in **phases** to manage risk and investment, and to allow learning and adjustment at each stage. There are three main phases envisioned, each with specific goals:

### Phase 1: Initial Deployment

**Phase 1** focuses on proving the core concept with a minimal footprint. In this phase, a small yard at the port is prepared and **one to two compute pads** are deployed, populated with tenant servers. The local hydroelectric connection is established: the **village substation goes live**, receiving power from the hydro plant via the short MV line, and supplying the first pad(s). The **fiber trunk is laid and lit up**, bringing connectivity to the site, and the NOC becomes operational to manage network traffic. The first pad(s) are connected to this fiber link, and initial “public drop” connections are set up (for example, the school or clinic in the village might get a high-speed internet connection from the project’s fiber).

On the thermal side, Phase 1 establishes the **energy center and primary heat loop infrastructure**. This includes installing the **plate heat exchangers** that tie the IT cooling loop to the building heating loop, and laying insulated piping to a few initial heat clients. **Public buildings are the first heat customers** – typically the community buildings like the clinic, school, and town hall are connected in Phase 1. If those buildings require higher temperatures, the **heat pump booster** is also installed in Phase 1 so that their heating needs can be fully met. A **stratified thermal storage tank** (or a small bank of tanks) is put in place to provide about \~6 hours of heat storage, which helps manage daily peaks. Additionally, the groundwork for the **greenhouse** is laid – possibly a small pilot greenhouse (a “greenhouse stub”) is built or an existing greenhouse is retrofitted to accept heat. Even if the greenhouse won’t need heat until summer, having the infrastructure ready in Phase 1 allows testing of the concept.

By the end of Phase 1, the project should demonstrate **basic functionality**: green compute is running in the village, useful heat is flowing to buildings, and data is being exported via fiber. Several go/no-go **gate criteria** must be met at this stage to proceed. **Fiber connectivity must be proven reliable**, with the trunk meeting its SLA (high availability and low latency in practice) and an automatic failover test showing that a pad’s dual links can lose one link without losing connectivity. The **local power system must be fully commissioned and safe** – the substation and pad feeders energized with all protections tested (e.g., verifying that if a pad draws too much power, only its breaker trips). The **cooling and heat reuse system must operate as designed**, meaning the **non-contact cooling works with no leaks and the ΔT on the environmental discharge stays within limit** during tests. Initial **pad leases should be signed** and importantly they should include the heat-first clauses so that even the first tenant is contractually on board with the reuse concept. On the community side, **FPIC should be formally concluded with consent given**, and the **Community Benefit Agreement (CBA/IBA) signed** and active before the project moves to full operation. Furthermore, the **governance committees are seated** (Project Council, Heat Committee, etc. have members appointed and have had initial meetings by the end of Phase 1). Finally, a **public dashboard** or scorecard should be online, displaying at least the essential indicators (even if in pilot form) to honor the transparency commitment from day one. Meeting all these criteria gives the green light to scale up to Phase 2.

### Phase 2: Expansion

In **Phase 2**, the project scales up both its compute capacity and its heat distribution reach. Additional **compute pads are added** to the port yard as new tenants lease capacity. For example, if Phase 1 had 1–2 pads, Phase 2 might add a few more (depending on demand, perhaps bringing the total to 4–5 pads). The electrical infrastructure (substation, feeders) was already sized with this in mind, so new pad connections are relatively straightforward – they are hooked into the existing substation and distribution panels, with new meters for each.

On the heat reuse front, Phase 2 extends the **district heating loop to nearby housing** and possibly additional public or commercial buildings. After covering the main public buildings in Phase 1, now the focus is on **connecting clusters of homes** that are close to the loop (for practicality and cost-effectiveness). This involves laying more twin-pipe distribution lines and installing heat exchanger substations in those homes. The capacity of the heat system is also increased: **thermal storage** might be expanded (e.g., adding another tank or two if the heating load has grown), and if a booster was not installed in Phase 1 (because it wasn’t needed for the initial buildings), it might be installed now if some new loads require higher temperatures.

Phase 2 likely sees the **greenhouse scale up** significantly. A larger greenhouse, or multiple greenhouses, are built to utilize summer heat at a scale that matches the increased number of pads (and thus more waste heat in summer). This could involve building a bigger structure or upgrading an experimental greenhouse from Phase 1 into a full-scale operation capable of year-round or at least three-season growing. The greenhouse can become a community enterprise, improving local food security (there’s reference in documentation to subarctic greenhouse self-sufficiency efforts) and providing jobs.

The **fiber network** in Phase 2 connects more local facilities. Additional community buildings (maybe a community center, more schools or a small college campus, a fishery co-op, etc.) get **fiber drops** so they benefit from better internet. With more pads online, the outbound data capacity is also increased – DWDM channels can be added to the trunk to handle more bandwidth as needed, which is an incremental upgrade. **End-to-end failover** should be fully proven by now; for instance, by Phase 2 the project should demonstrate that even if the primary fiber route to the south goes down, a secondary link (if available) can keep the site connected (this might involve things like a microwave backup link or a second fiber through a different path). Phase 2 is about **operationalizing redundancy**: making sure the systems put in place truly provide uninterrupted service as the scale grows.

By the end of Phase 2, Kristal Farms should approach a steady operational rhythm. Many of the **operational kinks are worked out** and the focus shifts to optimization. For example, **QoS tuning** on the network might be refined as usage patterns become clear (ensuring interactive traffic gets through even when training jobs are using bandwidth). Maintenance procedures are fine-tuned with the experience from Phase 1. The governance committees by now have a couple of cycles of meetings under their belt, and any needed adjustments in governance may be made (perhaps committees expand, or certain decisions are delegated differently based on Phase 1 experience – e.g., if it was found that the Project Council needed more local voices, adjustments could be done in Phase 2).

Key criteria to move to Phase 3 would include successful integration of new pads without loss of performance. For instance, ensuring that even with more IT load, the **HUF (Heat Utilization Factor) remains high** because the extended heat network and greenhouse are absorbing the extra heat (i.e., not more than a minimal fraction of heat is being dumped). It also includes verifying that the **fiber network can handle the increased traffic** and that the path protection works (if not already fully tested in Phase 1). The **community uptake** of benefits should also be visible by now – e.g., local employment targets being met, training programs producing skilled operators, and community satisfaction remaining positive (this might be assessed via FPIC review or a town hall at the transition to Phase 3).

### Phase 3: Full Campus and Steady State

**Phase 3** is the culmination of the Kristal Farms vision – the project reaches its full intended scale. In this phase, the site becomes a **full compute campus**: the remaining **pad slots in the yard are filled out with containers**, reaching the planned capacity (for example, if the design was for up to, say, 8–10 pads, Phase 3 would bring the total to that number). This modular growth means that by Phase 3, Kristal Farms is hosting a significant amount of compute (possibly tens of MW of IT load if each pad is a few MW), all of it powered by the local hydro plant.

The **district heating network** is fully extended to cover as many buildings as planned in the community. Potentially, additional loops or branches might be added to reach farther houses if economically feasible, and **larger buildings or new constructions** (like any new housing developments or a small industrial facility) are integrated into the loop. At full scale, essentially **most of the village’s heating needs are supplied by the data center heat** in winter, drastically cutting or eliminating the use of heating oil or diesel in town. The community might also invest in upgrading building insulation or heating systems to better use the lower-temperature heat, further increasing HUF.

The **greenhouse** (or other heat-utilization projects like a fish farm or wood kiln, etc., if any) is fully operational with an area sized to take virtually all summer heat. For instance, if the greenhouse is, say, a multi-thousand-square-foot facility, it can use the servers’ heat for nearly 8 months of the year, growing fresh produce for the community and possibly export to nearby markets (note: the reference suggests subarctic greenhouse usage \~8 months with data center heat is feasible). Phase 3 could also explore additional heat uses – e.g., an aquaculture project or district hot water supply – depending on community needs, thus diversifying the portfolio of heat sinks.

By Phase 3, the **fiber and connectivity** aspect might also expand beyond just connecting to a single hub. If the model is replicated, Kristal Farms could become one node in a larger network of cold-hydro villages, potentially sharing data or balancing compute loads. But within this site, Phase 3’s network is in “steady state”: all intended community sites are connected (e.g., every public building might have fiber, maybe even home broadband in the village is served through the project’s trunk as a side benefit), and the NOC handles routine operations with a mature set of procedures. **Regular reports** are now routine, and the community is accustomed to seeing the metrics and participating in governance.

At this stage, the emphasis is on **optimization and knowledge transfer**. With the full campus running, the project can fine-tune PUE (maybe adding more free cooling strategies), further reduce any minimal diesel use (maybe the backup generator is hardly ever run and maintenance is the only operation), and **document the playbook** for replication. In fact, one of the outputs of Phase 3 is likely a replication guide for other sites (the documents hint at a “separate playbook” for replication and cross-site sharing of Kristals knowledge base). This means Phase 3 not only solidifies operations locally but also turns the project into a model that can be exported to other communities.

Acceptance at the end of Phase 3 (and for project completion) would check that all systems are meeting or exceeding targets. For example, confirm that **ΔT compliance has been maintained at 100% over long periods**, that the **HUF is above the promised floor even in summer** (meaning the greenhouse etc. did its job), and that **diesel usage in the community has dropped by the expected amount**, verified by records of diesel fuel deliveries over the years. Also, by this time **jobs and training commitments** should have been fulfilled – e.g., local operators are fully trained and maybe even running the NOC and energy center themselves, with the initiating company stepping a bit more into the background operationally.

In essence, Phase 3 is the **proof of concept at scale** – demonstrating that a full-scale data center campus can operate in harmony with a small community, to mutual benefit. It’s the template that can then be rolled out to other suitable locations (other cold regions with hydro power and a community that can use heat).

### Go/No-Go Acceptance Gates

Throughout these phases, **Go/No-Go gates** serve as formal checkpoints to ensure all critical conditions are satisfied before proceeding to the next stage or full operation. The major acceptance gates include:

* **Community Consent & Agreements:** Before initial power-on (end of Phase 1 readiness), it is mandatory that **FPIC is concluded with consent** and the **Community Benefit Agreement (or IBA)** is signed. In other words, the community’s explicit buy-in is a gate. If the community had withheld consent or negotiations failed, the project would not proceed. This gate ensures the social license to operate. Additionally, **committee structures must be in place** – the Project Council and other committees are seated so governance is ready to kick in from day one.

* **Infrastructure Readiness:** The physical infrastructure must be fully tested. The **village substation and all protection systems** should be commissioned and proven – e.g., relay trip tests, ground fault tests, etc., must all pass. Each **pad’s power feed and meter** should be tested under load. The **cooling system’s non-contact exchangers** must be tested at full temperature and flow to ensure no leaks and that the **ΔT guard mechanism is functioning** (the system can automatically regulate or alarm if temperature rise is too high). Essentially, **electrical, cooling, and pumping systems** have run under a simulated or actual load and met performance specs. Without this, you wouldn’t add more pads or go live.

* **Network SLA Verification:** The **fiber network** is a gate to ensure compute can actually be exported. The acceptance criteria are that the **trunk link achieves the promised availability and latency** in practice (often this might mean running a burn-in period where you measure uptime over a month or two to see it meets ≥99.9% for example). Furthermore, each pad’s **dual uplink failover** is tested to demonstrate seamless switch-over (usually done by intentionally unplugging one link and observing traffic continuing). Also, it must be shown that **tenant vs community traffic are securely segregated** – for instance, a test that a device on the tenant network cannot reach a device on the community network, confirming VLAN or firewall separation. And finally, the **public network dashboard** should be live with these metrics visible, confirming that monitoring is in place.

* **Heat-First Operations:** Before scaling up, it should be demonstrated that the **heat reuse system is effective**. Acceptance tests include running the system and showing that the **Heat Utilization Factor is above a minimum level** – for example, in winter near 100% if all heat goes to buildings, or in a mild season hitting whatever threshold was agreed (to prove that the concept works). The **heat-first logic must be metered and active**: this means you can show at any given time whether heat is being reused, stored, or rejected, and that the control system is following the intended priority. Another aspect is verifying the **heat supply to buildings meets requirements** – e.g., conduct a test on a cold day to ensure building interiors reach their set temperatures with the server heat (possibly with booster assist). If any building can’t get enough heat, that’s a no-go until resolved (perhaps adding insulation or increasing capacity). Essentially the community should see that *their homes/buildings are warm and the servers are doing it*. Additionally, if the design included a **social heat quota** (a portion of heat guaranteed for public buildings), the system should demonstrate that allocation working as planned.

* **Safety and Compliance:** All **safety audits** (electrical, fire, etc.) must be passed. For electrical: grounding verified, fault clearance times within safe limits, etc.. For mechanical: pressure tests on pipes, emergency shutdown procedures validated. Also a **cybersecurity audit** might be included to ensure the black-box data separation is effective (no unintended data leakage). Furthermore, a **Reversibility Plan** must be on file – meaning there is a documented plan for how the pads would be decommissioned and removed, and how the site would be cleaned up, if the project ends. This is a gate to ensure long-term environmental and community protection; it shows the commitment that the host will restore the site if needed.

* **Operational Transparency:** Another gate item is launching the **public dashboard with essential indicators**. This is less a technical requirement and more a governance one, but it is often included: before saying the project is fully operational, the promised transparency system (dashboard) should be live. It demonstrates good faith and also is an early check that data collection systems are working (because to display PUE, HUF, etc., you must be measuring them correctly). Similarly, the **committees should have had at least their initial meetings** to validate that the governance mechanism is active.

Only when all these acceptance criteria are met, a **Go** decision is given to proceed to the next phase or to enter commercial operation. If any criterion is not met, the project would hold at that gate (for example, delay Phase 2 until the fiber failover works properly, or hold off adding more pads until heat usage is tuned up to target). These gates align with both technical milestones and community process milestones, reflecting the dual nature of Kristal Farms as an infrastructure project and a community partnership.

### Reversibility and Site Restoration

A key principle embedded in Kristal Farms’ design and agreements is **physical reversibility**. The entire data center installation is treated as non-permanent and removable, such that if the project is terminated or at lease end, the **pads can be taken away and the site can be returned to its original state**. This is quite different from a conventional data center that might pour a lot of concrete and erect large buildings. Here, the compute units are modular and transportable.

On a practical level, the **compute pads (containers)** can be lifted by crane back onto a truck or barge, the same way they were delivered. The pad yard itself – likely a graded gravel or concrete platform – can be restored (gravel can be removed or re-used, concrete pads can be broken up and hauled off). The fencing can be taken down. **Underground pipes** for the heat loop can be dug out or left in place if agreed (since they are benign when not in use, sometimes old district heating pipes are just left, but the reversibility plan will specify which). **Cables and electrical gear** can be removed; the substation can be disassembled (especially if it’s a prefabricated unit or standard transformers that can be reused elsewhere). Within the lease contracts, it’s stated that upon exit, the host (or pad owner) is responsible for returning the site to baseline condition. “Baseline” would be defined (for instance, if it was an empty port lot, it needs to be an empty lot again, with any soil contamination remediated, etc.).

During operations, **maintaining reversibility** means avoiding any irreversible actions on the landscape. No large foundations that can’t be dug up, no chemicals that would leave lasting pollution, and preserving the existing infrastructure (like the hydro plant is an existing asset, and the port is existing – Kristal Farms is essentially an overlay on these). The **Reversibility Plan** document is prepared in Phase 1 and updated as needed, detailing how each component would be dealt with at end of life. It typically also ties into a **financial guarantee** or decommissioning bond that ensures funds are available to execute the removal when the time comes.

From a community perspective, this reversibility is important: it assures them that if the project doesn’t work out or after 10-20 years when servers are obsolete, the village will not be stuck with derelict infrastructure. Instead, they either get an upgraded project or the site goes back to how it was. It also puts some onus on the operator to keep community goodwill – since the community essentially has an easy out; if they withdraw support (after initial consent periods), the project can pack up with no harm left behind. This is somewhat analogous to how some wind farms or mines have decommissioning plans – here, even a high-tech data center has one.

In summary, **Kristal Farms is designed to be fully reversible**: *the host runs utilities and heat-first, tenants run compute, the community runs governance and benefits, and if anyone needs to stop, the hardware can be pulled out and everyone can walk away*. That “exit strategy” is part of risk management and is a reassurance to all parties that the project is flexible and responsible for its footprint.
