**Labrador Coast Hydro / Compute / Heat Project**  
**First target: Nain, Nunatsiavut**  
**Expansion logic: Labrador coastal communities**

**Document status:** Partner discussion draft  
**Intended audience:** Infrastructure partners, hydro/utility partners, compute tenants, telecom/fibre partners, community and government partners

## 1. One-page executive summary

Kristal Farms is a proposed Labrador coast infrastructure model that combines local renewable power, modular compute, fibre connectivity, and useful heat recovery into one community-scale platform.

The project thesis is simple:

> Co-locate compute with cold hydro, place modular data containers near village heat users, export compute results by fibre, reuse server heat locally, and operate tenant containers under a black-box model that protects tenant data while creating visible community value.

The first target is **Nain**, with future replication potential across Labrador coastal communities such as Hopedale, Makkovik, Postville, and Rigolet. Nain is the initial focus because it offers the right combination of strategic need, coastal access, local heat demand, diesel-displacement logic, and proximity to potential community-scale hydro development that can be studied further.

Kristal Farms is not a mega-dam project, not a long-distance power-export project, and not a conventional hyperscale data centre campus. It is a **coastal-accessible, community-scale infrastructure model** built around short connections, modular deployment, heat reuse, and phased proof.

### What Kristal Farms builds

Kristal Farms develops a village-adjacent compute and heat platform composed of:

- A local hydro / power integration strategy.
- A short medium-voltage connection to a village or port-area substation.
- A fenced modular compute pad yard using standard containerized units.
- Liquid or rear-door heat capture from compute equipment.
- Heat exchangers and a local heat loop serving public buildings, housing clusters, and seasonal greenhouse demand.
- Fibre connectivity and a network operations layer.
- Black-box tenancy contracts that separate host infrastructure operations from tenant data, models, software, and logs.
- A public metrics and governance framework focused on heat, reliability, community benefit, and environmental safeguards.

### Why this matters

Most data centre models treat heat as a waste stream and electricity as the product to move over long distances. Kristal Farms reverses that logic. It keeps electricity local, exports compute over fibre, and turns server heat into useful local energy.

For a Labrador coastal community, this can create several forms of value:

- Lower reliance on diesel heat and diesel electricity over time.
- New local infrastructure tied to clean power and fibre.
- Useful heat for schools, clinics, municipal buildings, homes, and greenhouses.
- Skilled local jobs and training pathways.
- A new revenue base from compute pad leases and infrastructure services.
- A replicable coastal model that can be expanded one pad and one community at a time.

### Immediate partner opportunity

Kristal Farms is seeking partners to validate and advance the first Labrador coast deployment pathway. The immediate need is not full-scale construction approval. The immediate need is structured alignment around:

1. Nain-first site confirmation.
2. Hydro and grid pre-feasibility.
3. Fibre and logistics feasibility.
4. Community and FPIC process design.
5. Pilot pad technical design.
6. Heat-user mapping and heat-loop feasibility.
7. Commercial structure and partner roles.

The first decision ask is to support a focused pre-feasibility and partner-alignment phase for a Nain pilot, with Labrador coastal replication treated as the second-stage expansion strategy.

## 2. Why Labrador coast

The Labrador coast presents a distinctive infrastructure opportunity: cold climate, coastal villages, diesel-replacement need, potential local hydro resources, marine logistics, and a strong case for community-scale infrastructure that does not depend on long high-voltage transmission corridors.

Kristal Farms is focused on **coastal Labrador** because the model requires four conditions to work together:

1. **Local or nearby clean power potential.**  
   The project needs a renewable energy source that can support phased compute growth without depending on distant grid expansion.

2. **Cold-climate cooling advantage.**  
   Cold air and cold coastal water reduce cooling burden and improve operating efficiency. The preferred design uses non-contact heat exchange and closed loops rather than evaporative cooling.

3. **Useful local heat demand.**  
   The compute modules must be near buildings, homes, or greenhouse loads that can use the heat. This is why the containers are placed near the village, not at a remote dam site.

4. **Fibre export path.**  
   The project exports compute results, not bulk electricity. Fibre availability and network resilience are therefore central to site selection.

This approach is intentionally different from inland mega-projects or large power-export concepts. Kristal Farms is not pursuing new mega-dams, long inland transmission corridors, or broad speculative development across the North. The project is focused on sites where modular compute, local hydro, coastal logistics, fibre, and community heat value can combine in a practical sequence.

### Labrador coast replication logic

The first deployment should prove a repeatable pattern:

```text
Local clean power + village-adjacent compute pads + fibre + heat reuse + community governance
```

Once validated in Nain, the same screening logic can be applied to other Labrador coastal communities. Each future site would still require its own hydro assessment, community process, fibre review, logistics plan, permitting pathway, and heat-user mapping.

## 4. What Kristal Farms builds

Kristal Farms is best understood as an infrastructure platform, not a single technology product.

The platform has seven practical components.

### 4.1 Local clean power integration

The project uses local hydro as the primary power basis. The preferred architecture avoids long high-voltage export lines. Instead, power is moved over a short medium-voltage connection to a village or port-area substation, then distributed to modular compute pads and heat-system equipment.

This structure reduces the need for long transmission corridors, major remote substations, and large up-front infrastructure commitments.

### 4.2 Village-adjacent compute pad yard

Compute containers are placed near the village heat users, preferably near the port or village edge, not at the remote hydro site. This keeps heat close to public buildings, homes, and greenhouse loads.

The pad yard would include:

- Prepared modular pads.
- Power handoff and metering.
- Cooling and heat-exchange interfaces.
- Fibre ports.
- Physical security.
- Maintenance access.
- Space for phased expansion.

Each pad can be added, removed, upgraded, or replaced as demand and heat capacity evolve.

### 4.3 Containerized compute modules

The compute modules are standardized container units that can support different tenant configurations. Tenants may bring their own hardware or lease through a project-approved supplier model.

The site host provides the physical interfaces: power, cooling, heat export, fibre, and site security. The tenant controls its own equipment, workloads, software, data, and models.

### 4.4 Heat capture and local heat loop

Nearly all IT power consumed by servers becomes heat. Kristal Farms treats that heat as a local asset.

The heat system follows a simple dispatch rule:

```text
Reuse → Store → Reject
```

First, heat is reused in public buildings, homes, domestic hot-water preheat, and greenhouse loads. Second, excess heat can be stored in short-duration thermal tanks. Third, only remaining heat is rejected through approved non-contact cooling systems or dry coolers.

The preferred system uses two sealed circuits:

- An IT cooling loop for the tenant container.
- A building / community heat loop for heat users.

The loops exchange heat through plate heat exchangers. Fluids do not mix.

### 4.5 Cooling system

The project avoids conventional chiller-heavy data-centre cooling wherever possible. The preferred cooling architecture uses direct liquid cooling or rear-door heat exchangers, closed-loop circulation, and non-contact heat exchange to a cold coastal source or dry-cooler backup.

Cooling design priorities are:

- Low power usage effectiveness.
- Near-zero water consumption from evaporative cooling.
- No contamination between IT fluids, building-loop water, and the environment.
- Environmental discharge safeguards.
- Redundant cooling path for safe curtailment or shutdown.

### 4.6 Fibre and network operations

Kristal Farms exports compute results by fibre. Fibre is therefore not a secondary utility; it is core infrastructure.

The site requires:

- A network operations node.
- Fibre backhaul to regional connectivity.
- Pad-level fibre interfaces.
- Monitoring of availability and latency.
- Tenant and community traffic separation.
- Operational procedures for outages and maintenance.

The host does not inspect tenant payloads. Network monitoring is limited to infrastructure-level metrics such as link status, bandwidth volume, availability, and latency.

### 4.7 Governance, scorecard, and audit framework

The project must be measurable and accountable. Public-facing metrics should include energy efficiency, heat delivered, heat utilization, diesel avoided, fibre availability, uptime, local jobs, training hours, and community benefit indicators.

Governance should include community participation, FPIC process design, benefit-sharing mechanisms, and clear distinction between confirmed principles and open design items.

## 6. Site-selection logic

Kristal Farms uses a practical screening framework. A site is attractive only if it can support the whole system, not just one component.

### Core screening criteria

1. **Coastal or practical marine access**  
   Equipment should be deliverable through realistic seasonal logistics. The project should not overpromise year-round marine access.

2. **Local clean power potential**  
   The site needs a credible renewable energy source and a feasible short power connection.

3. **Village heat demand**  
   There must be priority users close enough to justify a heat loop: clinic, school, municipal buildings, housing clusters, greenhouse site, or other local heat loads.

4. **Fibre path**  
   Compute export depends on fibre availability, upgrade potential, redundancy, and service-level expectations.

5. **Community alignment**  
   The project must be developed through a legitimate community process, not imposed as a remote infrastructure project.

6. **Environmental feasibility**  
   Hydro, cooling, heat rejection, pad siting, marine logistics, and construction impacts must be reviewed through appropriate studies.

7. **Modular expansion potential**  
   The site should allow phased pads rather than requiring a large first build.

### Exclusion logic

Kristal Farms should exclude sites or concepts that depend on:

- New mega-dams.
- Major inland transmission corridors.
- Unvalidated large reservoir impacts.
- Remote compute pads too far from useful heat users.
- Fibre assumptions that cannot be verified.
- Community benefit models that are not locally governed.
- Economics dependent on speculative revenue claims.

## 7. Community and heat value

Heat reuse is not an add-on. It is the central difference between Kristal Farms and a conventional data centre.

### Heat-first operating principle

The operating principle is:

```text
Public and community heat value first.
Compute output second.
Heat rejection last.
```

This means that the system is designed around local heat users from the beginning. Compute capacity should grow in phases as useful heat demand, fibre capacity, and community alignment are confirmed.

### Priority heat users

Initial heat users should be selected based on public value, proximity, technical feasibility, and reliability needs.

Likely priority users include:

1. Clinic / health facilities.
2. School and education buildings.
3. Municipal or community buildings.
4. Nearby housing clusters.
5. Domestic hot-water preheat.
6. Greenhouse or food-production facilities.
7. Thermal storage tanks.

### Seasonal heat strategy

Winter: public buildings and nearby homes take priority.  
Shoulder seasons: heat is split between buildings, storage, and greenhouse use.  
Summer: greenhouse and storage become the primary useful sinks, with rejection only when useful options are saturated.

### Community benefit logic

The community value comes from several channels:

- Useful heat delivered.
- Diesel avoided.
- Lower exposure to fuel logistics.
- Better local air quality from reduced combustion.
- Jobs and training.
- Community-benefit agreements.
- Local procurement where practical.
- Potential fibre improvements.
- Transparent public reporting.

## 8. Black-box tenancy and data boundaries

Kristal Farms separates physical infrastructure hosting from tenant compute operations.

The host provides:

- Power.
- Cooling interface.
- Heat export interface.
- Fibre connection.
- Pad security.
- Physical metering.
- Environmental and uptime monitoring.

The tenant controls:

- Servers.
- Data.
- Models.
- Software.
- Workloads.
- Application logs.
- Internal telemetry.
- Encryption and access controls.

### What the host may monitor

The host may monitor physical infrastructure metrics, including:

- kW and kWh.
- Voltage and power-quality events.
- Cooling flow and temperature differential.
- Heat exported.
- Pad uptime or heartbeat status.
- Bandwidth volume.
- Fibre availability and latency.
- Technical alarms.

### What the host does not access

The host does not access:

- Tenant data.
- Tenant models.
- Tenant application logs.
- Tenant code.
- Tenant packet payloads.
- Sensitive network metadata beyond aggregate infrastructure metrics.
- Internal workload content.

Optional hardware attestation or confidential-compute requirements can be added for tenants that require stronger technical assurances.

Kristal Farms should be developed as a community-first project, not merely as infrastructure placed near a community.

### Confirmed principles

The partner-facing governance position should include the following confirmed principles:

- FPIC process required.
- Community benefit structure required.
- Heat allocation priorities must be locally informed.
- Public metrics should be reported regularly.
- Environmental safeguards must be measurable.
- Local jobs and training should be part of the model.
- Governance details must be developed with the community and relevant public authorities.

### Open design items

Several governance items should remain open until partner and community discussions occur:

- Final committee composition.
- Which committees are advisory vs. decision-making.
- Heat allocation rules during constrained periods.
- Dispute escalation process.
- Community fund mechanics.
- Revenue-sharing structure.

## 14. Closing thesis

Kristal Farms is a Labrador coast infrastructure proposal built around a practical inversion of the conventional data centre model.

Instead of exporting electricity over long distances, the project keeps clean power local and exports compute by fibre. Instead of rejecting server heat, it routes that heat to buildings, homes, storage, and greenhouses. Instead of building a large one-shot campus, it grows by modular pads. Instead of asking the host community to accept a black-box project, it gives compute tenants black-box data protection while making the physical benefits visible through heat, jobs, training, metrics, and governance.

Nain is the first test of that model. Labrador coastal replication is the expansion logic.

The next step is a focused partner-alignment and pre-feasibility phase to determine whether the Nain pilot can be validated technically, commercially, environmentally, and through the required community process.
